## Textbook

### The question: what is one example? {#statement}

A certificate learner simulates or records a system from several starting points, stores each trajectory at many times, and fits a candidate certificate. The statistical results of this chapter are stated for $n$ independent examples ([[risk-erm]]). Before any of them can be applied, we must say what one example is, what its loss is, and what $n$ counts. The answer: one example is one **whole trajectory**, generated by one independently drawn initial state, and its loss asks whether the certificate condition fails **at any time** on that trajectory. Recording more times along a trajectory does not add examples. It does make a separate, numerical question matter: whether checking finitely many times can certify all times.

**Recalled from [[dynamical-systems]].** A fixed closed loop $\dot x=f(x)$ with state $x\in\mathbb R^p$ has flow $\phi_t(\xi)$: the state at time $t\ge0$ of the solution that starts at the initial state $\xi$. For the scalar loop $\dot x=-x$, $\phi_t(\xi)=e^{-t}\xi$. The flow is **deterministic**: once $\xi$ is fixed, the entire trajectory $t\mapsto\phi_t(\xi)$ is fixed.

**Recalled from [[risk-erm]].** Examples $Z_1,\dots,Z_n$ are i.i.d. draws distributed like a generic example $Z$. A hypothesis $\psi$ has loss function $f_\psi$, risk $Pf_\psi=\mathbb Ef_\psi(Z)$ and empirical risk $P_nf_\psi=\frac1n\sum_if_\psi(Z_i)$.

**The certificate condition.** A candidate certificate is a function $V$ of the state, with gradient $\nabla V$. Its defining inequality is written as a **residual** that should be at most $0$ at every state the system visits:
$$h\big(x,\ \dot x,\ V(x),\ \nabla V(x)\big)\le0.$$
For example, a decrease condition asks $V$ to fall along trajectories at least at rate $\rho(V)$, where $\rho$ is a fixed increasing function with $\rho(0)=0$: $h=\nabla V(x)^{\mathsf T}\dot x+\rho(V(x))$. Along a trajectory, $\nabla V(x(t))^{\mathsf T}\dot x(t)$ is $\frac{d}{dt}V(x(t))$ by the chain rule. In one dimension with $V(x)=x^2$ it is $2x\dot x$. Boffi et al. state the goal as this inequality for every initial state in a set $X$ and every time, their equation (3.1) (arXiv:2008.05952v2, §3.1, p. 4); they assume the time derivative $\dot x$ along recorded trajectories is available exactly, and compute it numerically in their experiments. The meaning of such certificates is developed in [[lyapunov-functions]] and [[learning-certificates]]; here only the residual's role as "should be $\le0$" matters.

### One trajectory, one example {#reasoning}

**Initial states and horizon.** Initial states are drawn from a distribution $\mathcal D$ on a set $X\subset\mathbb R^p$, and every trajectory is followed over the same time interval $[0,T]$, with $T$ fixed before the data. The data are $n$ initial states $\xi_1,\dots,\xi_n$ drawn **independently** from $\mathcal D$, together with their trajectories.

::: definition {#def-traj-max} Trajectory residual and violation probability
The **trajectory residual** of $V$ at initial state $\xi$ is the worst residual along the trajectory:
$$\begin{aligned}H_V(\xi)=\max_{t\in[0,T]}h\big(&\phi_t(\xi),\ f(\phi_t(\xi)),\\&V(\phi_t(\xi)),\ \nabla V(\phi_t(\xi))\big).\end{aligned} \label{traj-max}$$
The candidate **violates** on $\xi$ when $H_V(\xi)>0$, that is, when the residual is positive at some time. The **violation probability** of $V$ is
$$\operatorname{err}(V)=\Pr_{\xi\sim\mathcal D}\big(H_V(\xi)>0\big).$$
:::

The maximum exists when the residual is continuous along the trajectory, because $[0,T]$ is closed and bounded; on an unbounded interval it is replaced by a supremum. Boffi et al. write $h(\xi,V)$ for $H_V(\xi)$, reusing the letter of the pointwise residual, and \ref{traj-max} is their equation (3.3) with a finite horizon (arXiv:2008.05952v2, §3.1, pp. 4–5; their $T$ is the time interval itself). [[boffi-thm-4-2]] uses their notation.

**The dictionary.** In the language of [[risk-erm]] (notation note: $f$ alone is the closed-loop vector field; the loss function of [[risk-erm]] is written with a subscript, $f_V$; and $X$ here is the set of initial states, not the input of a classifier):

- the example is the initial state, $Z=\xi$, drawn from $\mathcal D$;
- the hypothesis is the certificate $V$, and the class is the set of candidates fixed before the data;
- the loss function is the violation indicator $f_V(\xi)=\mathbf 1\{H_V(\xi)>0\}$, so the risk is $Pf_V=\operatorname{err}(V)$;
- the empirical risk $P_nf_V$ is the fraction of the $n$ sampled trajectories on which $V$ violates somewhere.

With a margin $\gamma>0$, the learner instead requires $H_V(\xi_i)\le-\gamma$ for every $i$, which is Boffi et al.'s problem (3.2) ([[margin-bounds]]).

**Why the count is trajectories.** The $n$ initial states are independent, and each trajectory, with every quantity computed from it, is a fixed function of its own initial state. Functions of independent random variables are independent, so the $n$ losses $f_V(\xi_1),\dots,f_V(\xi_n)$ are independent, as concentration and union-bound arguments require. Times along *one* trajectory are not independent of each other: they are all determined by the same $\xi_i$.

::: example {#ex-count} Twenty initial states, six hundred times each
A dataset contains $20$ initial states drawn independently from $\mathcal D$, each trajectory recorded at $600$ times: $12{,}000$ state records. The number of independent examples is $n=20$.

Using $12{,}000$ would overstate the evidence by a factor of $600$. In the zero-error bound for a finite class ([[finite-classes]]) with two candidates and $\delta=0.05$, the guarantee for a candidate that is feasible on every trajectory is $\log40/n$. With the correct $n=20$ it is $0.184$; with $12{,}000$ it would be $0.00031$.

For the scalar loop $\dot x=-x$ with $\xi$ uniform on $[-2,2]$, the indicator "the state is positive at time $t$" equals $\mathbf 1\{e^{-t}\xi>0\}=\mathbf 1\{\xi>0\}$ at every time. Its $600$ recorded values are $600$ copies of one fair coin toss, and averaging them is no more informative than looking at the toss once ([[concentration]] shows what Hoeffding would wrongly claim for copies).
:::

**What finer recording does buy.** More recorded times make the computed maximum in \ref{traj-max} closer to the true one, a numerical improvement for each example. They do not change $n$. Two other practices also do not add independent examples: restarting a simulation from the end state of a recorded trajectory, which by the composition rule $\phi_{s+t}(\xi)=\phi_t(\phi_s(\xi))$ only extends the same trajectory; and cutting one trajectory into segments.

**Holdout trajectories.** Testing a fitted certificate on fresh trajectories works because the certificate is fixed with respect to them. If $m$ holdout initial states are drawn independently of the training data and $V$ violates on none of them, then for $V$ with violation probability $q$ this outcome has probability $(1-q)^m$. Setting $(1-q)^m=0.05$ gives the one-sided $95\%$ upper limit $q=1-0.05^{1/m}$: if the violation probability exceeded this value, seeing no violation in $m$ independent tries would have probability below $0.05$. The limit is $0.0295$ for $m=100$. This is the finite-class argument with a single candidate; the cruder bound $\log20/m=0.0300$ comes from $(1-q)^m\le e^{-qm}$. The atlas Lab ([[certificates-in-practice]]) draws $100$ holdout initial states from a random stream separate from the training stream and prints this limit when no holdout trajectory violates. The limit concerns the Lab's own certificate, fitted with a relative margin, and its own initial-state distribution.

### Checking all times from finitely many {#time-grid}

A computer checks the residual at finitely many times. Let $r(t)$ be the residual along one trajectory, the expression maximized in \ref{traj-max}, and let the grid be $t_j=j\Delta$ for $j=0,1,\dots,N$, with spacing $\Delta=T/N$. The **grid maximum** $\max_jr(t_j)$ can only be smaller than the true maximum $H_V(\xi)$, because it maximizes over fewer times.

**The grid can miss a violation.** Take, for illustration, $r(t)=-0.1+0.3\sin^2(\pi t/\Delta)$. At every grid time $\sin(\pi j)=0$, so the grid sees $-0.1$ everywhere and reports a margin of $0.1$. Halfway between grid times $r=0.2>0$: the condition fails at every midpoint. No number of trajectories detects this, and refining the grid helps only if the refinement does not oscillate along with it.

::: lemma {#lem-bridge} A Lipschitz-in-time bridge
Suppose $|r(t)-r(s)|\le L_t|t-s|$ for all $s,t\in[0,T]$. Then
$$\max_{t\in[0,T]}r(t)\le\max_{0\le j\le N}r(t_j)+\frac{L_t\Delta}2.$$
:::

::: proof
Every $t\in[0,T]$ lies in some interval $[t_j,t_{j+1}]$ of length $\Delta$, so it is within $\Delta/2$ of its nearer endpoint $t_k$. Then $r(t)\le r(t_k)+L_t|t-t_k|\le\max_jr(t_j)+L_t\Delta/2$. Take the maximum over $t$.
:::

So a grid check certifies all times if the grid residuals are at most $-(\gamma+L_t\Delta/2)$: the true trajectory residual is then at most $-\gamma$. The constant $L_t$ must hold for every candidate in the class and every trajectory, and it is computed from bounds on the dynamics and the certificate: by the chain rule $r'(t)$ involves $f$, its derivative, and the first and second derivatives of $V$ along the trajectory. In the oscillating illustration $L_t=0.3\pi/\Delta$, and the lemma's bound is $-0.1+0.3\pi/2\approx0.371$, correctly refusing to certify.

::: example {#ex-bridge} The bridge for $\dot x=-x$
Take $V(x)=x^2$ and the decrease residual $h=\nabla V(x)\dot x+\rho(V(x))$ with $\rho(v)=v$. Along $x(t)=e^{-t}\xi$,
$$r(t)=2x(-x)+x^2=-x^2=-\xi^2e^{-2t}.$$
Let $\mathcal D$ live on $1\le|\xi|\le2$ and $T=1$. The residual increases toward $0$ in time, so its maximum is at $t=1$: $H_V(\xi)=-\xi^2e^{-2}\le-e^{-2}\approx-0.135$. Its time derivative is $r'(t)=2\xi^2e^{-2t}$, at most $2\cdot2^2=8$, so $L_t=8$ works for every allowed $\xi$. With grid spacing $\Delta=0.01$ (101 times), $L_t\Delta/2=0.04$. The grid residuals are at most $-0.135$, so the lemma certifies $H_V(\xi)\le-0.135+0.04=-0.095$ for every trajectory, and a fixed margin $\gamma=0.09$ is certified from the grid alone. Here the grid contains the maximizing time $t=1$, so the bridge is conservative by exactly $0.04$; its value is that it needs no knowledge of where the maximum lies.
:::

The fixed margin is attainable here only because every trajectory stays away from the equilibrium on $[0,1]$: $|x(t)|\ge e^{-1}$. If $\mathcal D$ gave positive probability to every neighborhood of $0$, then for any $\gamma>0$ fixed in advance a positive fraction of trajectories would have $H_V(\xi)=-\xi^2e^{-2}>-\gamma$ (those with $\xi^2<\gamma e^2$). The chance that all $n$ sampled trajectories meet the margin would then shrink to $0$ as $n$ grows. On an unbounded horizon the residual $-x^2$ tends to $0$ along every trajectory, and the margin fails on all of them ([[margin-bounds]]).

The atlas Lab checks its training constraints at every tenth point of its numerical trajectories (spacing $0.2$ s over a $3$ s horizon) without a bridge, so its sampled feasibility is a statement about those times only. A recorded trajectory is also itself a numerical approximation; integration error is a further gap that the bridge does not cover.

### Assumptions and domain {#assumptions}

- **Independent initial states from the deployment distribution.** $\operatorname{err}(V)$ is defined for $\mathcal D$. States chosen adaptively, reused or restarted from earlier trajectories are not independent draws.
- **A common horizon fixed in advance**, on which every trajectory exists and stays bounded, so that \ref{traj-max} is a well-defined random variable.
- **Count trajectories.** $n$ is the number of independent initial states; time samples improve the numerical maximum only.
- **All times, not grid times.** The loss uses the maximum over $[0,T]$. Certifying it from a grid needs a uniform Lipschitz constant in time and a grid margin $L_t\Delta/2$.
- **Holdout independence.** A holdout bound applies to a certificate fixed before the holdout states are drawn.

### Count and bridge {#questions}

::: question
A learner draws $8$ initial states independently from $\mathcal D$ and records each trajectory at $300$ times. It also restarts $2$ simulations from the final states of earlier trajectories, and cuts every trajectory into $3$ time segments, treating each segment as an example. How many independent examples from $\mathcal D$ does it have?
---
$8$. The $300$ times are determined by each initial state. The restarted simulations continue earlier trajectories (composition rule), and their starting states are not drawn from $\mathcal D$. Segments of one trajectory are functions of the same initial state.
:::

::: question
Along every trajectory the residual is $3$-Lipschitz in time, the grid spacing is $\Delta=0.02$, and every grid residual is at most $-0.05$. What does \ref{lem-bridge} certify? What spacing would certify a trajectory residual of at most $-0.035$, assuming the grid residuals at the finer spacing are still at most $-0.05$? (A finer grid can only raise the grid maximum, so this must be checked again.)
---
$-0.05+3\cdot0.02/2=-0.02$, so every trajectory residual is at most $-0.02$. For $-0.035$ we need $-0.05+1.5\Delta\le-0.035$, that is $\Delta\le0.01$.
:::

## Card

### The idea {#idea}

One independent initial state $\xi\sim\mathcal D$ gives one example: the whole trajectory, scored by its worst residual $H_V(\xi)=\max_{t\in[0,T]}h(\phi_t(\xi),f(\phi_t(\xi)),V,\nabla V)$. The loss is $\mathbf 1\{H_V(\xi)>0\}$ and its risk is $\operatorname{err}(V)=\Pr_{\xi\sim\mathcal D}(H_V(\xi)>0)$. Boffi et al. write $h(\xi,V)$.

### Intuition and example {#intuition}

A deterministic trajectory is a function of its initial state, so its time samples are copies of one draw. $20$ initial states $\times$ $600$ times gives $n=20$, not $12{,}000$. A grid can miss a violation between times; if the residual is $L_t$-Lipschitz in time, grid residuals at most $-(\gamma+L_t\Delta/2)$ certify $H_V\le-\gamma$. For $\dot x=-x$, $V=x^2$, $\rho(v)=v$, $1\le|\xi|\le2$, $T=1$: $L_t=8$, $\Delta=0.01$ certifies $H_V\le-0.095$.

### Assumptions and domain {#assumptions}

I.i.d. initial states from the deployment distribution; common horizon fixed in advance; bounded trajectories; uniform Lipschitz-in-time bound for grid certification; holdout states independent of fitting.
